# Life OS — Manifesto

---

## Preamble

Every piece of software ever built waits for you.

It sits behind an icon. It holds your things in the exact state you left them. It does nothing
until you arrive, and it never once wonders where you've been. We call this "a tool," and we
have accepted for fifty years that the price of a tool is that **you must remember to pick it
up**.

That price was invisible while computers couldn't think. It isn't anymore.

This document is about what becomes possible when the thing you were using to remember can
remember on its own.

---

## I. What Life OS is

**Life OS is the first software that takes custody.**

Not storage. Custody.

Storage is a place to put something. Custody is someone accepting responsibility for it. Every
productivity tool ever made offers the first and quietly refuses the second. Your todo app holds
your task the way a shelf holds a book — perfectly, indefinitely, and without the faintest
interest in whether it is ever read again.

Life OS is a correspondent, not a container. You hand it something, and it becomes *theirs* to
carry. It works while you are elsewhere. It comes back to you only when your judgment — not your
labor, not your attention, not your memory — is genuinely the missing ingredient.

Three verbs. That is the entire operating system.

> **You hand over. It holds. It asks.**

Everything else — the models, the agents, the artifacts, the memory, the whole apparatus — is
plumbing in service of those three words.

---

## II. Why it deserves to exist

Because the burden was never the work.

The burden is *holding* the work. Knowing it exists. Knowing it isn't done. Knowing roughly when
it will become urgent. Carrying that low hum in the back of the mind through dinner, through
sleep, through a conversation with someone you love. Psychologists have a clinical name for the
open loop; everyone else knows it as the thing that wakes you at 3am with the word *"did I—"*.

No tool has ever taken that. Not one. They took the *filing*, which was never the hard part.

We can take it now. That is the whole reason to build this. Not efficiency — efficiency is a
metric for factories. **Relief.** The product's success is measured in things you have stopped
carrying.

If Life OS does not make a person lighter, it has no reason to exist, no matter how impressive
it is.

---

## III. What every productivity tool fundamentally fails to solve

They make you the clerk of your own life.

Look honestly at the best of them. Things, Todoist, Notion, Linear, Asana, Obsidian — genuinely
beautiful work, all of it. And every single one requires that you:

- remember the system exists
- open it
- put things into it, correctly, in its vocabulary
- return to it on a rhythm to keep it true
- and *trust* it, where trust is manufactured by your own labor

Getting Things Done — the most rigorous personal system ever designed — is explicit about this.
Its central promise is "a trusted system," and its central mechanism is the **weekly review**.
That is the confession written into the doctrine: the system is trustworthy *only if you
maintain it*. You may put down the tasks, but you may never put down the reviewing.

**You never actually hand anything over. You just move it to a shelf you're still responsible
for dusting.**

This is not a flaw in those products. It is the ceiling of what software could do before it
could think. A filing cabinet cannot notice that your friend's father died and that you should
call. It can only hold a card that says so, if you wrote one.

The ceiling is gone. Almost nobody has noticed, because everyone is busy putting a chat box on
the filing cabinet.

---

## IV. The inversion

Here is the structural claim of this product, and it is one sentence:

> **In all existing software, the human asks and the machine answers.
> In Life OS, the machine asks and the human answers.**

That is the whole invention.

Every tool you have ever used is retrieval-shaped: you arrive with a question, you dig, you
extract. Even chatbots — the newest thing we have — are retrieval-shaped. You still initiate.
You still hold the thread. You still remember to come back.

Life OS reverses the direction. It carries the loops. It does the work it can do. And when it
reaches a fork that genuinely requires a human — taste, values, risk, the shape of your life —
it comes and asks. Once. Clearly. At a moment you can actually think.

**The atomic unit of this product is not a screen, a record, a task, or a chat message. It is a
question worth asking.**

Everything upstream — the memory, the reasoning, the agents, the artifacts — exists for one
purpose: to produce a small number of excellent questions and to have already done everything
that didn't require one.

This gives us the strangest quality metric in software: **the product gets better as it speaks
less.** A version that asks four excellent questions a week is superior to one that asks forty
good ones. We are building the first system whose ambition is to need you rarely.

Which means we are building against the grain of every business model in consumer technology.
Good. That's how you know it's worth doing.

---

## V. Should anyone open Life OS?

**No. Opening it is a failure.**

If you must open it, you must remember to open it — and remembering is precisely the burden we
claimed to take. An app you must check is a task with an icon.

So Life OS has no home screen in the sense the phrase usually means. There is no lobby, no
dashboard, no board of eight rooms waiting to be tended. It arrives: a line on a lock screen, a
question at a moment when you can answer it, a small confirmation that something you'd forgotten
was handled while you were asleep.

There is exactly one place you *can* go, and it exists only so that trust can be verified:

**The Ledger.** Everything currently held, everything done, and — crucially — *why*. Not a
workspace. Not a place work happens. An audit surface. You go there to check that the system is
what it says it is, and the design goal is that you stop needing to within a month, the way you
stopped checking whether your calendar really remembers your appointments.

A workspace is where you labor. A ledger is where you verify. Building the second and refusing
to build the first is the most consequential decision in this document.

---

## VI. What we are deleting

Everything below is optional, and we are choosing to live without it.

**Apps.** An app is a room you must enter to do a kind of thing. The kinds are ours, not yours;
your life doesn't decompose along vendor lines. Life OS has no apps, including inside itself.

**The home screen.** A grid of doors is an artifact of a machine that couldn't decide what
mattered. Ours can. What matters comes to you.

**Teams.** Organisational structure is how *we* built it, not how you experience it. You will
never navigate to a team. You may meet the agent that did a thing, by name, at the moment it
matters — attribution, not architecture.

**Missions, projects, tasks, folders, tags.** Containers you must choose before you may speak.
Every one of them makes you classify your intent for the machine's convenience. Classification
is the machine's job now; that is the entire premise of building on top of intelligence.

**Documents, as a required form.** Sometimes the right output is a document. Often it is a
decision, a reservation, a message sent, a thing quietly prevented. A product that can only
produce documents will produce beautiful documents nobody acts on.

**The inbox.** An inbox is a pile of unprocessed obligation with a number on it — the burden,
handed straight back, wearing a badge. Life OS never accumulates a queue for you to work
through. It asks one thing at a time, or it says nothing.

---

## VII. What it should feel like after one year

Not "productive." Productivity is a word for people who are being measured.

The feeling is **being well-kept.**

It is the feeling of a household that runs — where the bills were paid, the birthday was
remembered, the appointment was moved when the meeting ran long, and nobody told you about any
of it because none of it required you. Not the pride of having managed your life well. The
quieter thing underneath: **not having had to.**

Concretely, after a year:

- You have stopped keeping a mental list. Not "you keep a shorter one." **You stopped.**
- The 3am *"did I—"* has gone quiet, and you can no longer remember exactly when it stopped.
- You are mildly surprised, three or four times a month, by something handled that you'd have
  been embarrassed to forget.
- You trust it the way you trust that your photos are still there — without ever having decided
  to.
- When it asks you something, you feel *respected*, not interrupted, because it has never once
  asked you something it could have worked out itself.

The end state is that Life OS becomes uninteresting to you. You will not think about it. That is
not a failure of engagement; it is the product working exactly as designed. **We are building
something to be forgotten by the person it serves, and remembered only when they try to live
without it.**

---

## VIII. What must never exist inside Life OS

These are not preferences. They are foreclosed.

- **No badge, streak, count, or score.** Nothing that manufactures obligation. The moment a
  number goes up, we have started charging rent on the user's attention.
- **No feed. No infinite scroll. No session length as a metric.** We do not want more of the
  user's time. We want less of it, and we should be willing to say so in public.
- **No accusatory emptiness.** No "0 completed." No untended rooms. The absence of activity in
  some part of your life is not a moral failure and will never be rendered as one.
- **No settings as a substitute for judgment.** A preference pane is a confession that we
  couldn't decide. Boundaries you set, yes. Forty toggles, never.
- **No black boxes.** Every action the system takes must be openable: what it did, why, what it
  used, what it assumed. If the reasoning cannot be shown, the action cannot be taken.
- **No irreversible act without a question.** Anything that spends money, sends words to another
  human, or cannot be undone is always, always an ask.
- **No theater.** No fake thinking animations, no agents performing labor for the user's
  entertainment. If work is invisible, we say so plainly. Watching software pretend to work is
  the most expensive kind of nothing.
- **No shame.** Ever. In any copy, in any state, in any nudge. There is a great deal of money in
  making people feel behind. We will not take it.
- **No weekly review.** If the system requires ritual maintenance to stay trustworthy, we have
  rebuilt the thing we set out to replace.

## IX. The one law

Every feature, forever, faces one question:

> ### Does this take something off the user's mind, or put something on it?
>
> If it puts anything on their mind — a thing to remember, to check, to maintain, to configure,
> to feel behind on — **it does not ship.** However valuable it is. However much they asked for
> it.

This is deliberately brutal. It kills most good ideas, including ideas that would increase usage,
retention, and revenue. It kills the dashboard. It kills the notification you'd love to send. It
kills the settings screen you're sure people want. It kills the beautiful visualization of
everything the system has done for them.

It has to be brutal, because the failure mode of this product is not building something bad. The
failure mode is **building something good that gradually becomes one more thing to keep up
with**. That has happened to every tool in this category, without exception, and it happened one
reasonable feature at a time.

One law, applied without mercy, is the only known defense.

## X. If Jobs, Ive, Victor and Kay had to agree

Put them in a room and you get a real fight, not a consensus. It's worth being precise about the
fight, because the resolution is the product.

**Jobs** would demand one thing, unreasonably simple, with an emotional claim you could say in a
sentence to your mother. He'd cut until one thing remained, and he'd cut the part everyone loved
most. His sentence here: *"It takes the worry."*

**Ive** would want it to nearly disappear. No ornament, no chrome, honest materials, no form
beyond what function demands. He'd push hardest on the thing we've already decided: it should
mostly not be on a screen at all. He would want the moment it does appear to be *inevitable* —
as though it couldn't have arrived any other way.

**Victor** would object, loudly, to everything above. His life's argument is that hiding a
system's workings is how we get people who can't think with their tools. A machine that decides
quietly is a machine you cannot see, question, or reason alongside. He would demand that the
thinking be *visible and touchable* — that you can open any conclusion, see the values that
produced it, change one, and watch the outcome move.

**Kay** would go further still: it must be a **medium, not an appliance**. His standing
complaint about modern software is that it's a vending machine — you get what the vendor
imagined and nothing else. He'd insist you can teach it. Not configure it. *Teach* it, by
example, in the course of ordinary use, so that the system you have after a year is genuinely
yours and could belong to no one else.

The fight is real: **Jobs and Ive want it invisible. Victor and Kay want it visible.**

The resolution is not compromise. It's a sequence:

> **Invisible by default. Inspectable on demand. Teachable by anyone who looks.**

The system acts quietly and leaves a trace. The trace is never in your way, and is always one
gesture away. Opening a trace shows you not just what happened but *the reasoning that produced
it* — and here is the part that makes Kay and Victor sign: **correcting the reasoning is how you
teach it.** Not a settings screen. You disagree with a conclusion, you say why, and the thing
that changes is not one outcome but the standing judgment behind it.

So what would the four of them build?

**A quiet, honest correspondent that holds the open loops of your life, acts only inside
boundaries you can see, asks you only what only you can answer — and becomes more yours every
time you disagree with it.**

That is a product none of the four could have built alone, and it is the one this company should
build.

---

## XI. What we are afraid of

A manifesto that names no danger is marketing. Three real ones.

**Atrophy.** A system that remembers for you may leave you unable to remember. There is a version
of this product that produces capable, dependent people — and Victor's objection is the sharpest
form of the warning. Our answer is the trace: the system never hides what it concluded or why,
and it hands back the *judgment* while absorbing the *bookkeeping*. We must watch for the day
those two blur, because they will.

**Intimacy without consent.** Some of what this system would touch involves other people —
messages to your mother, plans with your friends — who never agreed to be handled. Delegating a
résumé is not the same act as delegating a relationship, and any product that treats them as one
domain with one interaction model deserves the recoil it will get. Some things must remain
manual not for technical reasons but because doing them yourself *is the point*.

**Becoming what we replaced.** Every tool in this category began as relief and ended as
obligation, one reasonable feature at a time. The law in section IX exists because we already
know we will be tempted, and we know exactly what the temptation will sound like: *"users are
asking for it."*

---

## XII. The promise

We are not building a better way to manage your life.

We are building the first thing in the history of software that will **carry** part of it — and
give it back only when your judgment is genuinely required.

The measure is not features shipped, time in app, or tasks completed.

The measure is a single sentence, said by a real person, a year in:

> **"I don't have to remember that anymore."**

Everything that serves that sentence, we build.

Everything else, no matter how good, we don't.
